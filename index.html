Foundations — the spine

Eleven ideas explain most architectures in this library. Master these and new chips stop looking like alphabet soup — they become variations on a theme.

01

Pipelining & speculation

Split work into stages, keep every stage busy, predict branches and execute out of order — then undo cleanly when you guessed wrong.

02

Parallelism shapes

ILP in a CPU core, SIMD in a vector unit, SIMT across GPU threads, and spatial dataflow in an AI array are different bets on where parallel work lives.

03

Memory is the architecture

Caches, scratchpads, registers and HBM stacks exist because moving a byte often costs more energy than computing on it.

04

Dataflow & systolic arrays

Keep weights or pixels stationary and stream activations past them. Reuse is designed into the wiring, not hoped for from a cache.

05

Heterogeneity

General cores for control, fixed-function blocks for codecs and crypto, programmable accelerators in between. The scheduler becomes part of the architecture.

06

Interconnect & NoC

As dies grew, the wires became the computer: meshes, rings, NVLink, Infinity Fabric and CXL decide who starves and who flies.

07

Chiplets & packaging

Build big systems from small, high-yield dies. UCIe, HBM stacks and 3D stacking moved architecture decisions into the package.

08

Precision as a feature

FP64 for science, FP16/BF16 for training, INT8/FP8 for inference, fixed-point for pixels. Choosing numbers is choosing silicon area.

09

Design for Test (DFT)

A chip you cannot test is a chip you cannot ship. Scan chains, ATPG, BIST, boundary scan and compression turn internal nodes into controllable, observable points — at a small area cost paid back in yield learning.

10

Low-power architecture

Clock gating stops waste, power gating stops leakage, DVFS trades voltage for frequency, and multi-Vt trades speed for leakage. Power intent (UPF) makes it a design contract, not an afterthought.

11

Bandwidth, latency & PPA

Silicon succeeds on a triangle: bandwidth feeds it, latency hides or exposes stalls, and power/performance/area (PPA) decides if it can be built, cooled and sold. Optimise one in isolation and the others punish you.

Building blocks — the periodic table of silicon

Strip any chip in this library down and you find the same ~20 primitives, recombined. Architectures differ not in whether they have caches, queues and multipliers — but in which primitive they bet the die on, how big they make it, and what they delete. Learn the blocks and every “new” architecture becomes a familiar sentence in a new order. Filter by family, then tap any catalogue card. Entries marked ✓ chip-specific name the documented blocks inside that exact design, with the special piece starred; the rest show the standard pattern for their class and are labelled “class pattern” — a representative anatomy, never dressed up as that chip's die inventory.

How to read a block: Job = what it does every cycle. Cost = what you pay for it in area, energy or latency. The special piece in a great design is usually a block whose cost everyone else refused to pay — until the workload proved them wrong.

Silicon success — beyond compute

Great FLOPS on a slide mean nothing if the chip starves, overheats, cannot be tested, or yields badly. Every serious entry in this compendium should be read against these six factors.

BANDWIDTH

Feed the compute

Memory bandwidth (HBM/LPDDR), NoC bisection bandwidth and link bandwidth (NVLink, PCIe, Ethernet) set the ceiling. Quote GB/s or TB/s per watt — not just peak.

LATENCY

Hide it or remove it

Cache hit latency, NoC hop latency, SerDes latency and memory latency decide real performance on dependent, irregular and interactive work. Prefetch, speculation and locality are latency tools.

POWER / PPA

The shipping constraint

Dynamic power ≈ α·C·V²·f; leakage never sleeps. PPA is negotiated with clock/power gating, DVFS/AVS, multi-Vt, body bias and dark-silicon budgeting — unused silicon you cannot power all at once.

TESTABILITY

Prove it works

Fault coverage, test time and test cost are architecture. Scan, BIST, IJTAG and hierarchical DFT decide ATE time, DPPM and how fast yield ramps.

YIELD & DFM

Design for manufacturing

Design-for-manufacturing rules, redundancy, ECC, repair and binning turn a good design into a profitable one. Larger dies and 3D stacks amplify every defect decision.

RELIABILITY

Survive the field

Aging (NBTI/HCI), electromigration, thermal cycling, soft errors and in-field test (SLT, burn-in, periodic BIST) — especially for automotive and data-centre silicon.

Timeline — five pivots

The field didn’t evolve in a straight line. It lurched each time the bottleneck moved.

1971–1995 · The CPU era

4004 to Pentium Pro: the race was single-thread performance — deeper pipelines, caches, superscalar and out-of-order execution.

1996–2006 · Graphics becomes compute

Programmable shaders turned GPUs from fixed rasterisers into parallel computers. Dennard scaling ends; multicore begins.

2007–2016 · Mobile & heterogeneity

The smartphone SoC fused CPU, GPU, ISP, modem and codec blocks. Performance per watt replaced raw gigahertz as the headline.

2012–2020 · The AI accelerator explosion

Deep learning made matrix multiplication the world’s most valuable operation. TPUs, Tensor Cores and wafer-scale engines followed.

2020–today · Chiplets, HBM & domain systems

Monolithic dies hit reticle and yield limits. The frontier moved to packaging, memory bandwidth, interconnect — and full-system co-design.

Glossary — in plain language

OoO — Out-of-order execution. The core reorders instructions to keep units busy while respecting true dependencies.
SIMT — Single Instruction, Multiple Threads. GPU model where groups of threads execute in lockstep on different data.
MAC array — A grid of multiply-accumulate units; the beating heart of most AI accelerators.
NoC — Network-on-Chip. The packet-switched fabric connecting blocks inside a large die.
ISP — Image Signal Processor. Turns raw sensor Bayer data into a viewable photo: demosaic, noise reduction, tone mapping.
VCU / VPU — Video processing hardware for encode/decode of H.264, HEVC, VP9 and AV1 at a fraction of CPU energy.
HBM — High Bandwidth Memory. Stacked DRAM on an interposer beside the die, trading capacity for enormous bandwidth.
DFT / ATPG — Design-for-Test / Automatic Test Pattern Generation. Scan + ATPG creates patterns that detect stuck-at, transition and cell-aware faults.
BIST — Built-In Self-Test. On-chip pattern generation and response analysis for logic (LBIST) and memories (MBIST with repair).
DVFS / AVS — Dynamic Voltage & Frequency Scaling / Adaptive Voltage Scaling. Closed-loop control that runs each domain only as fast — and as high-voltage — as needed.
UPF — Unified Power Format (IEEE 1801). The power-intent file declaring domains, isolation, retention and level shifters.
VOQ — Virtual Output Queue. Switch buffering at ingress per destination, avoiding head-of-line blocking without a giant central shared buffer.
PAM4 / SerDes — 4-level pulse-amplitude modulation over a serialiser/deserialiser link: 2 bits per symbol, at the cost of signal integrity and FEC latency.
DPU — Data Processing Unit. Offloads networking, storage and security work from host CPUs in data centres.

How to break down any new chip announcement

Don't read the headline specs — deconstruct it. Ask nine questions: 1) What are its base building blocks — name the compute, memory, movement and control primitives. 2) Which single block is the special piece, and what cost did everyone else refuse to pay for it? 3) What workload is it shaped for? 4) Where does data live and how far does it travel per operation? 5) What precision does it compute in? 6) What is fixed-function vs programmable? 7) What do the package and memory system allow that the die alone could not? 8) How is it powered down, clock-gated and voltage-scaled when idle? 9) How is it tested — scan, BIST, boundary scan — and what coverage and test time result? If a launch doesn't answer those, it's marketing, not architecture. Every entry in the catalogue above is organised to answer exactly this breakdown — chip-specific where the anatomy is documented, and labelled as a class pattern where it is not.
